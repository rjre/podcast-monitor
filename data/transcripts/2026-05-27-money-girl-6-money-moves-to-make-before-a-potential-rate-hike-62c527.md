If you've been waiting for interest rates to drop before making a big financial move like buying a home or a car, sticky inflation is making a rate hike more likely this year. While higher rates can be bad news for borrowers, there are upsides if you know how to leverage them. In this podcast, I'll review six smart money moves you shouldn't overlook when interest rates and inflation go up. These tips will make your money work harder and help offset rising prices. Hey everyone, welcome back to episode 1222 of Money Girl. I'm your host, Laura Adams. I'm an award-winning author, spokesperson and money speaker. And in the show notes, you can find links to learn more about me and how to reach out. Alright, let's talk about how interest rates and inflation work together. You probably know that the Federal Reserve or the Fed is the central bank of the United States and it was created by Congress in 1913. While its job is pretty complex, you can really break it down into two main goals. Its job is to maintain a healthy economy by maximizing employment and keeping prices stable. Now the Fed doesn't directly set the prices of things like groceries and gasoline or the interest rate on credit cards and mortgages. It does control the Federal Funds rate. That's the interest rate that banks charge each other. And from that, the interest rates that you and I pay sort of trickle down from that. And when the economy is sluggish and inflation is relatively low, the Fed lowers rate rates to make borrowing and spending more attractive to consumers and businesses. For instance, when it costs less to finance a car, you might be more likely to buy one, right? But when inflation is high, the Fed raises rates to slow down the economy. If it costs more to finance a car, you might delay buying one. So by cooling consumer and business spending and demand, prices will eventually come down to encourage more spending. Right now, inflation in many categories of goods and services is stubbornly high. We kind of tribute much of it to global issues like the Iran War, which has caused oil and gas prices to rise significantly. The cost of energy affects just about every product we buy because shipping gets added to its price. So you might think about the Fed as the economy's thermostat. When it gets hot with prices rising too quickly, the Fed will turn up the interest rates to cool it down. While higher interest rates certainly have their downsides, like paying higher rates on credit cards, auto loans and mortgages, there are some upsides and strategic moves you should make. So we're going to go through six of them. Number one is to create or update your personal financial statement or PFS. Now if you're a regular money girl listener, you've definitely heard me talk about creating a PFS. And this is a simple tool that I created for you. And it's for just simply tracking your net worth over time. That's important because it gives you a macro view of your financial health and wealth. And you can download my free PFS template when you sign up for my substack newsletter called The Money Stack. And you can do that by going to my website, loradatoms.com or just doing a search for The Money Stack. And again, it's free and the newsletter is also free. Now you can also create your own PFS document using paper, a word, doc, or a spreadsheet. Your net worth equals your assets. These are things like your bank accounts, vehicles, and homes minus your liabilities like mortgages, student loans and credit cards. Basically it's what you'd have left over if you sold all of your valuables and paid off all your debt. For instance, if you have $300,000 in total assets and $250,000 in total debts, your net worth is $50,000. Now you can also owe more than you own giving you a negative net worth. So knowing the big picture of your finances helps you build wealth because it helps you understand what to focus on. So when interest rates rise, understanding the terms of your liabilities is really important. So all of your debts, your mortgages, car loans, student loans, personal loans, credit cards, all of that, it's important for you to understand what's going on with those debts. And when you list them out on your PFS, you get to really understand them because you're going to include the outstanding balance, the interest rate, and whether it's a fixed or variable interest debt. Having all this information in one place gives you a holistic view of your finances, and it allows you to make the best decisions, especially in a rising rate environment. Okay, and the second money move is to pay down your variable rate debt. Once you have everything listed out in a personal financial statement, it becomes very clear what you need to focus on. Now having a fixed rate loan with a payment that never changes, that's a huge benefit when interest rates rise. But as I mentioned, your variable rate debts, things like credit cards, home equity lines of credit, and adjustable rate mortgages, they're going to rise when rates increase. So paying down your variable rate debts in order of highest to lowest interest rate is usually the best strategy, and that's because you're going to save the most in interest. Move number three, use a balanced transfer offer. If you have balances on high rate credit cards, the rates probably going to increase as interest rates go up. Now if you can't eliminate your high rate credit cards, consider a balanced transfer credit card offer that typically locks in a 0% promotional rate for approximately 12 to 18 months. And that might be available on a credit card you already have, or you may need to look for a new card. While using a transfer card certainly doesn't make your debt go away, but it insulates you from rising interest rates for a period. You've got the opportunity to save a lot of interest and use that savings to help pay off your balance faster. In most cases, even after paying a fee, such as a 5% transfer fee on the amount that you transfer from an existing debt to a new card or a new balance transfer offer, you can still save money. Move number four, don't delay necessary large purchases. If you're considering a big purchase, maybe it's a home or a car, waiting could be costly. That's because higher interest rates increase the cost, and more rate hikes could be on the way. While you can't perfectly time a significant purchase, when it's possible, lock in the lowest fixed rate loan as quickly as you can. When you're in a rising rate environment, money is getting more expensive to borrow, so waiting simply means paying more. But that being said, I don't want you to panic and make an expensive purchase just because interest rates are moving up. You know, it needs to be the right purchase for you. It's still got to fit into your budget and benefit your overall financial goals. If you plan to own a home for the long run, a fixed rate loan keeps your payment low even while interest rates are climbing. However, if you plan to sell the property within a few years, there's still a place for adjustable rate mortgages or arms. Just remember that with an arm, your interest rate and monthly payments can go up dramatically if you can't sell the property or refinance it down the road. Number 5. Maintain Good Credit Scores No matter what's happening with interest rates, your credit is a significant factor in how expensive it is to borrow money, so improving and maintaining good credit can really pay off. Plus a great score helps you save money in other ways, like paying less for auto insurance, home insurance, and utility deposits. And it can help you get approved or rent an apartment, receive premium offers, and even land a job with an employer that checks credit. Number 6. Maximize Your Savings Yield The biggest upside of higher interest rates is enjoying higher yields on your savings. However, you may need to move money to a dedicated, high yield savings account or HYSA. So keep an eye on your savings rate, and don't be afraid to shop around for better options. It's pretty easy to move your savings to a high yield savings account to earn more interest. I recommend that you maintain enough emergency savings to be safe in a crisis, like losing your job or your business income or having a significant unexpected expense. A good rule of thumb is to keep at least 3 to 6 months worth of your living expenses in a high yield FDIC-insured liquid savings account. It's going to keep your money extremely safe and allow you to tap it if and when needed. But be sure not to hold too much cash in savings. While earning 4% or more on a high yield savings account may seem great, remember that it gets offset by inflation. In other words, if inflation is 3.5%, and you earn 4% on your savings, the real return on that account is only half a percent. Now, that's okay because emergency funds are for safety, not for growth. But once you have a healthy emergency fund, you should invest excess cash for higher returns. Using a tax-advanced account like a workplace retirement plan, an individual retirement account or IRA, maybe you qualify for a health savings account or HSA, or you want to contribute to a 529 education savings plan. All of these accounts allow you to choose investments based on your risk tolerance and beat inflation with a higher real return and catch your taxes.
