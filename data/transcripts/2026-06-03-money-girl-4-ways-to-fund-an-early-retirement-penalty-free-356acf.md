If you're a regular money girl listener, you've heard me say that the official retirement age for tax-advanced accounts, like an individual retirement account or IRA, is 59 and a half. It's a very specific age. But what if you want to tap your hard-earned money before then? With drawing funds from a retirement account, early typically means paying a hefty 10% early withdrawal penalty, plus applicable taxes. However, the good news is that there are strategies and legal ways to crack open your retirement nest egg or even use other accounts if you want to retire early. So this podcast will review four ways to skip that dreaded penalty and still use your retirement funds anytime you like. Hey everyone, welcome back to episode 1024 of Money Girl. All right, let's talk about those four ways to fund early retirement penalty free. Whether you're ready to leave a high-pressure job or maybe you just can't work anymore due to physical or mental limitations or you want to pivot to a different lifestyle, early retirement maybe right for you. However, if you have investments in one or more tax-advanced accounts, they typically require you to exceed an age limit or pay that 10% early withdrawal penalty on distributions that I mentioned. But the good news is that I'm going to review several legal ways to tap your tax-advanced accounts as needed. The first is to use the rule of 55. The rule of 55 is a terrific escape hatch if you're an employee participating in a workplace retirement plan. It could be a 401k or a 403b or other types. This is an IRS rule that says if you leave your job for any reason during or after the calendar year you turn 55, you can start taking penalty-free distributions from that specific employer's retirement plan. However, any amounts not previously taxed would be subject to ordinary income tax, but you do get to skip that 10% penalty. Note that some company retirement plans may not allow the rule of 55 or they may not allow partial withdrawals. They may force you to withdraw your entire balance. That could potentially trigger a large tax bill. So check with your retirement plan's administrator or the custodian of the plan about what's allowed. If you can use the rule of 55, you don't even have to retire officially. This access is available whether you get downsized and just need additional income or maybe you resign to do part-time work with another employer or even you want to start your own business. As long as your separation from the employer sponsoring that retirement plan happens in the same year you turn 55 or later, you're eligible for penalty-free withdrawals. In fact, some public safety workers like police officers, firefighters and air traffic controllers may have the ability to take penalty-free withdrawals as early as age 50 or they could qualify after 25 years of service whichever comes first. So I want to reiterate that the rule of 55 or 50 only applies to your current workplace retirement plan. If you leave your employer and roll all those retirement funds into an IRA, you shatter the rule. The rule of 55 does not apply to IRAs or even a retirement plan with a previous employer. But stay with me, I'll cover ways that you can still tap an IRA early and penalty-free. The second way to retire early is having a Roth IRA. Roth accounts are great. They just come loaded with lots of benefits including tax-free account growth and withdrawals and retirement. While you do have to pay taxes upfront on your Roth contributions, you have the flexibility to take early withdrawals. With a Roth IRA, you can withdraw your original contributions at any age for any reason entirely tax and penalty-free. That's a great benefit. Once you exhaust withdrawals of original contributions, you can distribute amounts in a Roth IRA that were converted from a traditional IRA or rolled over from a traditional workplace retirement plan. However, they must have been in the account for at least five years to avoid the 10% early withdrawal penalty. Each separate conversion you do has its own five-year clock. But it's tax-free since it is taxed at the time of the conversion. Lastly, if you withdraw earnings from a Roth IRA before age 59.5, they are subject to income tax and the 10% penalty unless you qualify for an exemption. Some common IRA penalty exemptions include using up to $10,000 of lifetime earnings to buy or build a first home. Spending earnings on higher education expenses for you or a close relative and taking substantially equal periodic payments, which are known as a 72T, which we're going to talk about next. So number three is setting up a 72T payment plan. A 72T is an IRS rule that allows you to take equal distributions, like quarterly or annually, from a retirement account penalty-free. However, all distributions that weren't previously taxed are subject to taxes. You just can't get around those taxes. You can set up a 72T for a traditional IRA, a Roth IRA, or even a workplace plan with a previous employer. The amount you can withdraw with a 72T plan must be calculated using one of three accounting methods that are approved by the IRS. They all use factors like your account balance, age and life expectancy. The payment calculation can be based on the amount in a single retirement account or on the aggregate of all your retirement accounts. Once you begin taking 72T distributions, the catch is that you've got to continue those payments for a minimum of five years, or until you turn 59.5, which ever is longer. In other words, if you start a 72T at age 50, you would have to continue those payments for 9.5 years until you're 59.5. After you complete a series of five-year distributions or you reach age 59.5, you can take retirement distributions anyway you like. However, for most traditional accounts, once you reach age 73, and this will be higher, it will be 75, starting in 2033. You do have to take annual required minimum distributions or RMDs, whether you used a 72T plan or not. When properly executed, taking 72T payments can be a smart way to tap your retirement funds early without penalty. However, calculating the allowable payment schedule could be a little complicated, so I would encourage you to get help from a qualified tax professional when needed. Taking too little, too much, or missing a 72T distribution deadline can result in owing income tax and paying a 10% early withdrawal penalty, plus interest calculated from the original date you made an error. It could be pretty expensive if you mess it up. If you want to create a 72T, one strategy is to separate the funds you want to tap in a smaller IRA. That way, you only apply the plan on a portion of your retirement nest egg rather than on your entire balance. And the last way to retire early without penalty is to use a taxable brokerage account. While I'm a huge proponent of using tax advantage retirement accounts, there's also a place for taxable brokerage accounts in your planning. While they don't come with any tax perks, the upside of a brokerage is no restrictions on contributions or withdrawals. You can take brokerage distributions at any age and there are never penalties. That gives you a lot of flexibility to retire early if you wish. Plus, you pay capital gains taxes, and right now, those range from zero up to 20% on a brokerage account. So that could be lower than ordinary income tax rates, depending on your situation. But if you have a relatively low income, your capital gains rate could even be zero. While I don't recommend putting all your investments in a brokerage account, it can be used as a financial bridge from early retirement to age 59.5. You can freely access a brokerage until tapping your retirement account is penalty free. A good strategy is to hold tax-efficient investments like index funds and exchange-traded funds in a brokerage account because they trigger little annual tax. And you should own heavily taxed investments like actively manage mutual funds and high yield bonds in a tax-advanced IRA or workplace retirement plan. To sum up, the IRS discourages savers from tapping their retirement accounts early. However, it also allows ways for early retirees to avoid unnecessary penalties if they know how to navigate them. So if you're confident that you'll have enough wealth and future income, such as a combination of investments, a pension and social security benefits, to last a lifetime, tapping a retirement account early and penalty free may be an excellent goal.
