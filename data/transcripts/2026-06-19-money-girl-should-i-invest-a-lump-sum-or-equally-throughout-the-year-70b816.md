So if you invest $625 monthly to max out the account, that's using a DCA strategy. You could also invest about $144 a week or $1,875 a quarter to accomplish the same goal. DCA is commonly used for relatively volatile investments, like index, mutual, and exchange traded funds, where the price typically changes every time you intend to buy more shares. Here's an example of how that plays out. Let's say your investment is an index fund and it's trading at $100 a share. If you purchase $625 of the fund, you'd buy just over six shares. But if the fund increases in price to $120 the following month, you'd buy a little over five shares with that same amount of money. And if the price falls to $80 the next month, you'd buy about eight shares. The reality is you don't need any experience or expertise to implement a DCA strategy. In fact, if you're enrolled in a workplace retirement plan like a 401k or 403b, and you've got a flat amount or percentage of your paycheck contributed, you're already dollar-cost averaging. But what if you're like chip and you've got extra cash to invest in an IRA? You have the choice to invest it all at once or gradually by dollar-cost averaging. So let's talk about the pros of this DCA strategy. A primary benefit of DCA is that you reduce the risk of market volatility when you stick to the strategy over the long term. As an investment's price goes up and down, you smooth out how that affects your portfolio by being consistent. As I mentioned, by investing a consistent amount, you'll buy less when prices rise and more when prices drop. If you invest a lump sum when an investment's price is at an all-time high, you'd lose money and probably never have the chance to recover. But if you only invest a smaller amount when an investment is high, the price is high, your risk is much lower. Another benefit is that a DCA strategy is achievable if you don't typically have excess cash. Workplace retirement plans are so successful because they require participants to elect a percentage or a dollar amount from each paycheck to invest. And then you adjust your budget to live comfortably on the rest that you take home. I also think DCA makes investing less emotional for many people, especially if you put it on autopilot. Setting a reasonable annual goal and then dividing it into 12 monthly withdrawals from your paycheck or from your bank account helps you avoid thinking about the price of your investments. Once you choose investments like index or mutual funds, it doesn't matter what their prices are doing because you're committed to buying a pre-determined amount, whether they've gone up or down. By automating a DCA strategy, you stick to a set schedule and make investing a habit. DCA is best for investors with a long time horizon who don't have a large lump sum to invest or they don't want to worry about timing the market correctly because that is virtually impossible. All right, now let's talk about the cons of dollar cost averaging. I think the downside is that you could miss out on a huge increase in an investment's price compared to making a lump sum investment, especially if the investment is made when prices are low. Also, since the market generally rises over time, that's what we've seen historically, you can argue that investing a large amount earlier is better than investing smaller amounts over a longer period. In general, when the stock market is flat or declines, you're going to come out ahead with a DCA strategy. However, when the market increases, DCA may be a disadvantage.
